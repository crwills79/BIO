Profile

I work where analytics meets engineering: making the numbers in front of executives defensible, and making the systems that produce them durable. Most of what I deliver starts as a question nobody can answer confidently — a metric that looks wrong, a field that is silently empty, a pipeline that fails without telling anyone — and ends as a documented root cause, a fix routed to the team that owns it, and a check that catches the next occurrence automatically.

AI is central to how I do that, and it is not autocomplete. I own the domain reasoning, the data validation, and the judgment calls. AI removes the mechanical bottleneck — reading at volume, propagating a change across formats and layers, generating the queries that test my hypothesis. The result is enterprise-grade output at a pace that single-handed work cannot reach, with a human accountable at every gate that matters.

How I work with AI

Investigative

I define the hypothesis; AI generates the queries that test it. When the data contradicts the hypothesis — which is often the most valuable outcome — I decide the next direction. The pivots that shape a finding come from evidence and domain context, not from the model.

Comprehension

AI processes volumes of structured text that would take days to read manually — model definitions, pipeline logic, schema exports. I validate the output against what I know about the business and catch what is undocumented, misleading, or quietly wrong.

Execution

I define the transformation spec; AI propagates it systematically across files, formats, and layers that each have their own schema and syntax. I own correctness, feed errors back, and close the loop. Judgment stays the only limit on throughput.

The part people underestimate is the governance layer. Anything irreversible is gated on an explicit human decision. Checks fail closed rather than passing quietly. Queries are aggregate-first and privacy-safe by default. Findings are written down where they survive the session, so the next person does not rediscover them. Speed without those constraints is not an asset — it is just a faster way to be wrong.

Selected work

Data Quality Platform

Automated data-quality and lineage auditing

Reporting trust depended on somebody eventually noticing that a number looked wrong. Upstream schema changes, partial loads, and stalled refreshes reached stakeholders before they reached anyone who could fix them.

I built a daily audit that traces recursive upstream lineage across every object the reporting dataset consumes and surfaces only what is actually out of sync — schema drift, row-count divergence, freshness lag. Then I re-architected it off any dependency on an individual's machine into a fully server-side pipeline publishing to a cloud lakehouse. Settled-window logic suppresses false alarms from loads still in flight; a single configuration file is the source of truth; token refresh, write retries, and guards against partial-wipe states keep a mid-run failure from corrupting the result.

Outcome: silent upstream breakage became a detected, attributable finding — caught and routed before a stakeholder ever saw a wrong number.

Root-Cause Investigation

Reframing the question before spending effort on the wrong fix

The most expensive failure in analytics is a confident investigation of the wrong cause. Escalations arrive pre-diagnosed: a client's feed is blamed for a field that is actually populated by an internal warehouse process; a rate that spiked is assumed to be a real business shift; a count that "seems too low" is assumed to be a capture gap.

I read the lineage before pulling data, then design aggregate-only queries that make each hypothesis falsifiable. Repeatedly this has moved a problem to an entirely different owner: a reported client-data gap resolved to an internal pipeline that had never written the records at all; a leadership escalation over a spiking denial rate traced to a residual status code from a filter family fixed earlier; a suspiciously low subscriber count reconciled end to end and reframed as a definitional expectation, not a defect. When the data disproved my own theory, I reported that too.

Outcome: effort and remediation land on the real defect and the team that owns it, with evidence strong enough to survive challenge in the room.

AI Enablement

Turning expert workflows into reusable agent tooling

The workflows that made me effective — triaging incoming requests, planning and working a ticket backlog, producing a data-quality brief, mapping how a business flow moves through the data — all lived in my head and ran only when I ran them.

I generalized each into a client-agnostic, configuration-driven agent skill: discovery of the active context, deference to local rules and domain knowledge, and explicit approval gates before anything writes to a tracker or a channel. Advisor-only boundaries are enforced by design rather than by good intentions. I also built the attribution layer around it — reconciling tooling usage back to specific deliverables so the cost of AI-assisted work is auditable instead of asserted, and leaving unmatched activity unassigned rather than forcing a match.

Outcome: individual expertise became tooling colleagues can run, with the human checkpoints preserved and the spend defensible.

BI as Code

Semantic models and reports as version-controlled text

Traditional business-intelligence development is click-through. Every measure, visual, filter, and title changes in a desktop interface, cannot be code-reviewed, and cannot be reliably compared between versions. Errors are invisible until someone spots a wrong number in a meeting.

I author semantic model definitions and report layouts directly as text: measures and calculated columns, relationships and their active-versus-inactive behavior, row-level security, and report visual definitions with their query bindings and formatting. That makes model work diffable, reviewable, and scriptable — including repeatable transform scripts that apply a cleanup specification consistently instead of by hand. I have also reverse-engineered embedded queries out of published models into source-of-truth files so the logic can be reviewed, diffed, and shared.

Outcome: reporting gained the same rigor as the pipelines behind it, and an entire class of "someone clicked the wrong thing" errors disappeared.

Automation

Unattended, self-healing reporting operations

A recurring workbook refresh consumed skilled analyst time every morning: open, wait, trigger connections, save, eyeball the result. It also meant fresh data depended on a specific person being at a desk.

I automated the whole cycle on a schedule, then hardened it against the failure modes that only appear in production — refreshes reporting success instantly because the queries were running asynchronously, cloud-sync holding files locked at exactly the scheduled hour, orphaned application sessions, dialogs blocking an unattended run, and formatting silently lost on write. The pipeline now logs, backs up, retries with process cleanup, notifies a chat channel on success and failure, and recovers from transient faults on its own. When it went quiet for several days, the root cause turned out to be sync contention rather than the obvious suspect, and I fixed the cause instead of padding a timeout.

Outcome: a daily manual ritual became an unattended, self-reporting service with no single-person dependency.

Executive Communication

Translating technical remediation into a decision leaders can make

Technical wins die in translation. Leadership does not need the query; it needs to know what the reporting is for, what the risk was, what changed, what proves it, what keeps it working, what it cost, and where the limits are.

I build executive material as a connected argument in that order, with every slide title stating a business takeaway rather than a topic. I insist on labeled estimates over invented precision, on a break-even test rather than a fabricated return figure, and on stating the boundaries of a claim explicitly. The same finding gets delivered in layered versions — full technical detail for the system owner, filter and decision history for the analyst, and a version stripped of every table and column reference for the executive who has to act on it.

Outcome: technical investment gets understood and funded on its merits, and escalations close with a decision instead of another meeting.

Knowledge Engineering

Converting tribal expertise into institutional capability

In programs spanning multiple countries and data sources, undocumented business logic is operational risk. When the person who knows why a correction exists or how a reimbursement tier works moves on, the next analyst either reinvents it slowly or gets it wrong expensively.

I turn model exports, pipeline code, and scattered decisions into onboarding-grade references: architecture and data-flow documentation, field-definition dictionaries with source logic and availability, metric definitions with lineage, and investigation logs that record why a call was made. Domain knowledge is captured as a durable artifact at the moment it is discovered, not reconstructed later from memory.

Outcome: onboarding time drops, handoffs stop being risky, and the program can answer "how was this number calculated" under audit.

Collaboration & Enablement

Aligning definitions across functions, and lifting the people around me

Reporting accuracy is rarely blocked by a technical problem alone. It is blocked by legal, client-facing, engineering, product, and analytics functions holding different definitions of the same metric, across time zones.

I do the cross-functional alignment work that makes a definition stick, and I route fixes to the owning team with the evidence pre-assembled rather than escalating a complaint. Alongside that I mentor analysts into the models and pipelines — walking peers through security configuration and semantic-model connections, reviewing their work, and building the reference material specifically so colleagues can self-serve. I also flag limitations and caveats to stakeholders up front, before they become someone's surprise.

Outcome: capability scales past a single person, and stakeholders trust both the number and the caveats attached to it.

How I operate

  • Reframe before you fix. Read the lineage and challenge the premise of the request before spending a single query on the assumed cause.
  • Make every claim falsifiable. A finding is only useful if there is a specific test that would have disproved it, and I ran that test.
  • Privacy-safe by default. Aggregate-first methods, sensitive fields routed to count-only paths, identifier-level output written to controlled destinations only.
  • Report honestly, including against yourself. When the data disproves my own hypothesis, that is the finding, and it ships as-is.
  • Fix the cause, not the symptom. Padding a timeout, masking a bad value, or widening a filter buys quiet, not correctness.
  • Leave a durable artifact. Every investigation ends in documentation, a check, or tooling — not just an answer in a thread.
  • Gate anything irreversible on a human. Automation earns speed everywhere except the decision to commit, publish, or notify.
